1

What This Is

A complete standalone tabletop roleplaying game designed from the beginning for Foundry VTT.

This is not a conversion of D&D 5e, Pathfinder, Fabula Ultima, or another existing tabletop system.

It is not a recreation of the Honkai: Star Rail videogame with dice bolted onto it.

It is a new tabletop rules engine intended to capture the things that make a character-driven space-fantasy JRPG compelling:

  • Distinctive heroes with mechanically unique combat identities
  • Way and Element interactions
  • Shared party resources
  • Weakness and Break mechanics
  • Turn-order manipulation
  • Ultimates that can interrupt normal action flow
  • Follow-up attacks and reactions
  • Summoned entities and alternate combat states
  • Bosses with phase changes and encounter-specific rules
  • Exploration across dramatically different worlds
  • Towns and social spaces worth spending time in
  • Character relationships, downtime, rivalries, friendships, and nonsense
  • Missions ranging from cosmic crises to helping somebody around the corner
  • A campaign structure that allows the party to continually discover new civilizations, threats, and stories

Foundry is not merely the place where the game sheet happens to be displayed.

Foundry is part of the rules engine.

The system is allowed to track calculations, event chains, timing windows, resource changes, targeting restrictions, conditional abilities, status durations, and other mechanical interactions that would become cumbersome in a purely paper game.

The computer handles bookkeeping.

The players make decisions.

The GM runs the world.

2

The Core Fantasy

The player should feel like they are playing their own hero in an anime space-fantasy campaign, not controlling a spreadsheet and not impersonating an existing protagonist.

A campaign should comfortably support all of the following:

  • Traveling aboard a ship or other mobile home base
  • Arriving at an unfamiliar world
  • Wandering through a city
  • Sitting around a fountain talking nonsense with the party
  • Meeting NPCs
  • Shopping
  • Gambling
  • Investigating mysteries
  • Exploring ruins
  • Sneaking through restricted facilities
  • Getting caught
  • Talking their way out
  • Starting a bar fight
  • Having a completely unnecessary slap fight because someone insulted a character's favorite celebrity
  • Hunting monsters
  • Fighting soldiers, machines, strange lifeforms, and cosmic horrors
  • Saving a village
  • Becoming involved in faction politics
  • Developing friendships and rivalries
  • Returning to the ship after a mission
  • Spending downtime with companions
  • Upgrading gear
  • Developing new powers
  • Departing for the next world

The setting must support both:

“Tonight we kill the thing threatening this planet.”

and:

“Tonight we never left town.”

Both are legitimate sessions.

3

Primary Design Philosophy

3.1 The Tabletop Game Comes First

This must remain a tabletop roleplaying game.

The GM adjudicates an open world.

Players may attempt things the rules did not anticipate.

NPC conversations are conversations, not dialogue trees.

Exploration is not reduced to clicking videogame hotspots.

Characters are not confined to combat abilities.

The VTT supports imagination rather than replacing it.

3.2 The Computer Handles What Computers Are Good At

Players should not manually track:

  • Temporary modifier arithmetic
  • Action-value calculations
  • Break calculations
  • Resource triggers
  • Duration counters
  • Trigger chains
  • Repeated status damage
  • Follow-up eligibility
  • Reaction windows
  • Summon timers
  • Equipment-derived modifiers
  • Conditional bonuses
  • Multi-target damage distribution
  • Shared resource synchronization

If Foundry knows the answer, the player should usually not have to calculate it.

3.3 The Player Makes the Interesting Decision

Automation must never eliminate meaningful choices.

The engine may say:

Netherwing can react.

It should not decide whether Netherwing reacts.

The engine may say:

Ultimate Ready.

It should not fire the Ultimate automatically.

The engine may identify valid targets.

It should not pick the player's target unless the ability itself is explicitly random.

The guiding rule is:

Automate consequences. Preserve decisions.

3.4 Every Character Should Feel Different

Characters are not bags of different numerical bonuses wrapped around the same action loop.

Every hero is expected to possess a recognizable mechanical engine.

One hero may accumulate power from party HP fluctuations.

Another may hunt a marked target.

Another may manipulate the action timeline.

Another may store damage and release it later.

Another may deploy creatures or drones.

Another may change stance.

Another may consume their own health.

Another may intercept attacks.

Another may create battlefield rules that affect everyone.

Two characters following the same Way should still be capable of playing dramatically differently.

3.5 The Interface Is Part of Character Design

A character's special mechanic should not exist only as text.

If a character accumulates a unique resource, the resource appears.

If a summon enters battle, the sheet changes.

If a stance changes, the controls change.

If an Ultimate becomes available, the interface communicates it.

If an ability creates a reaction window, the appropriate player sees it immediately.

A player should be able to glance at the screen and understand:

  • Who they are
  • How healthy they are
  • What resources they have
  • What conditions affect them
  • What actions are available
  • What their character is currently trying to accomplish

3.6 Visual Spectacle Without Becoming a Videogame

Combat should feel dramatically different from ordinary Foundry combat.

However, this project will not attempt to reproduce a fully animated 3D videogame.

The visual language is:

2.5D tabletop battle presentation using flat character artwork, depth, scale, motion, effects, UI animation, and perspective.

Good staging is more important than expensive animation.

4

Three Modes of Play

The system has three major presentation states.

4.1 Exploration / Roleplaying Mode

Used for:

  • Towns
  • Ships
  • Wilderness
  • Dungeons
  • Investigation
  • Social scenes
  • Travel
  • Downtime
  • General roleplaying

The UI is deliberately lighter.

Players are encouraged to look at the world and one another rather than a combat HUD.

4.2 Combat Mode

When combat begins, the presentation changes.

The exploration interface gives way to:

  • 2.5D battle tableau
  • Combat timeline
  • Character resources
  • Party Skill Points
  • Target indicators
  • Action wheel
  • Enemy information
  • Weakness indicators
  • Break bars
  • Status feedback
  • Reaction prompts

The transition itself should feel significant.

The machine wakes up when violence begins.

4.3 Downtime Mode

Downtime is not merely “restore everything and skip ahead.”

It supports:

  • Resting
  • Hanging out
  • Personal projects
  • Research
  • Shopping
  • Crafting
  • Training
  • Bond scenes
  • Faction activity
  • Ship activity
  • Gambling and recreation
  • Personal errands
  • Medical recovery
  • Character development

Downtime mechanics should encourage roleplaying without replacing roleplaying.

5.1 Identity

Each character contains:

  • Name
  • Portrait
  • Level
  • Way
  • Element
  • Origin / Homeworld
  • Background
  • Role descriptors
  • Personal traits
  • Bonds
  • Faction affiliations
  • Notes / biography
  • Advancement state

Core Attributes

The exact mathematical implementation remains a prototype decision.

The initial design uses five broad Attributes:

AttributeGoverns
MightStrength, endurance, physical force
AgilityCoordination, reflexes, balance, stealth
IntellectReasoning, science, technology, investigation
PresenceCharm, intimidation, deception, social force
WillDiscipline, courage, supernatural resistance

These are tabletop attributes, not videogame combat statistics.

They provide the foundation for everything characters attempt outside their bespoke combat kits.

Final values, dice, modifier ranges, and advancement costs are intentionally TBD until prototype testing.

Initial categories may include:

  • Acrobatics
  • Engineering
  • Science

  • Insight

8

Core Resolution Engine

The final dice mechanic remains an early prototype decision.

Whatever resolution system is selected must satisfy the following requirements:

  1. Fast resolution
  2. Multiple degrees of outcome
  3. Easy Foundry automation
  4. Enough mechanical hooks for Traits and powers
  5. Useful failure without excessive whiffing
  6. Clear player-facing results
  7. Ability to resolve opposed and environmental challenges
  8. Minimal arithmetic at the table

Potential outcomes should distinguish more than simple success/failure.

The system should be capable of expressing concepts such as:

  • Failure
  • Success with complication
  • Standard success
  • Strong success
  • Exceptional / critical success

The final mechanic should be tested in actual play before becoming foundational.

9

Derived Combat Statistics

Combat statistics are derived separately from tabletop Attributes.

Potential core values include:

  • HP
  • Guard / Defense
  • Speed
  • Movement or Mobility where applicable
  • Break Power
  • Effect
  • Resistance
  • Critical capability if retained
  • Healing effectiveness
  • Shield strength

The goal is not to reproduce every Honkai numerical statistic.

Only statistics that create meaningful tabletop behavior should exist.

Every character possesses a recognizable combat kit built from common interface categories.

Basic Attack

Reliable attack requiring no party Skill Point.

Usually generates a party Skill Point.

Provides a default action when conserving resources.

Skill

The character's primary tactical action.

Usually costs party Skill Points.

Skills are expected to interact strongly with the character's signature mechanic.

Ultimate

A powerful ability charged through the character's own mechanics.

Ultimates are not restricted to the character's normal turn.

When available, they may be activated during legal timing windows and inserted into the action timeline.

Talent

The character's core passive or reactive engine.

Talents frequently listen for events such as:

  • Ally damaged
  • Enemy damaged
  • Healing
  • Shield gained
  • Shield broken
  • Weakness broken
  • Enemy defeated
  • Ultimate used
  • Skill used
  • Status applied
  • Turn beginning
  • Turn ending
  • Resource threshold reached

Technique

Primarily used:

  • Before combat
  • During exploration
  • At encounter entry
  • For ambushes
  • For environmental manipulation
  • To establish starting combat state

Techniques are intended to bridge exploration and combat.

Many characters possess something that goes beyond the five common ability categories.

Examples:

  • Summoned creature
  • Personal meter
  • Marks

  • Stored damage
  • Ammunition
  • Charges
  • Companion

  • Deployable object

  • Alternate action set

Party Skill Points

Skill Points are a shared party resource.

Example display:

● ● ○ ○ ○

Basic actions commonly generate Skill Points.

Skills commonly consume them.

Because the resource belongs to the party, it should exist primarily on the persistent party/combat HUD rather than inside any one character sheet.

All clients see the same synchronized value.

This creates cooperation without requiring conventional action-point bookkeeping.

13

Energy and Ultimates

Each character may possess an Energy meter or equivalent Ultimate charge mechanic.

Energy can be generated by:

  • Acting
  • Taking damage
  • Dealing damage
  • Healing
  • Character-specific Talents
  • Equipment
  • Ally actions
  • Enemy events

When the threshold is reached:

ULTIMATE READY

The system clearly alerts the owning player.

The player may activate the Ultimate during permitted timing windows.

14

Combat Timeline

Combat does not use traditional fixed-round initiative as its primary presentation.

Characters and enemies occupy a visual action timeline.

The engine tracks:

  • Current action order
  • Speed
  • Action advancement
  • Action delay
  • Extra actions
  • Summon turns
  • Interrupting Ultimates
  • Boss insertions
  • Follow-up actions

The player does not calculate timeline values manually.

When an effect alters action order, the portrait physically moves on the timeline.

15

Combat Presentation — 2.5D Battle Tableau

This is one of the project's defining systems.

Player Perspective

Each player client receives a composition centered around that player's own hero.

The player's character appears:

  • Large
  • Foreground
  • Center or center-right
  • Facing the enemy formation

Enemies appear primarily toward the left / middle distance.

Their apparent size and location communicate battlefield organization.

Nearby allies may appear partially off-screen and larger.

More distant allies may appear smaller.

The result creates depth without requiring actual 3D models.

Flat Character Art

Combatants use layered flat artwork.

Potential character states include:

  • Idle
  • Acting
  • Hurt
  • Ultimate
  • Defeated
  • Alternate stance
  • Summoned state

Not every character requires every state for the initial release.

Motion is created through:

  • Translation
  • Scaling
  • Parallax
  • Sprite swaps
  • Screen shake
  • Impact effects
  • Lighting
  • Particle overlays
  • UI transitions

The game should create the impression of motion without requiring expensive frame-by-frame animation.

16

Logical Battlefield Positioning

The visual battlefield is not a free-movement grid.

Combatants carry logical position data.

Enemy formation initially supports:

CodePosition
FRLFront Row Left
FRCFront Row Center
FRRFront Row Right
BRLBack Row Left
BRCBack Row Center
BRRBack Row Right

Internally, position should be stored separately:

row: front
lane: left

rather than relying on a single display string.

This allows rules to independently query Row and Lane.

Position Display

Position markers should not clutter normal play.

Hovering a target may show:

Murder Drone

FRL

Front Row — Left

or:

Kill Borg

BR

Back Row

Position Is Mostly State, Not Movement

Characters do not normally spend turns walking between grid squares.

Position changes occur when an ability or encounter rule makes them meaningful.

Examples:

  • Pull enemy Front
  • Knock enemy Back
  • Swap lanes
  • Expose Back Row target
  • Intercept
  • Push
  • Advance
  • Retreat
  • Protect corresponding lane

This provides tactical positioning without turning the game into miniature wargaming.

17

Targeting Types

The action engine must support at least:

Single Target

One valid target.

Blast

Primary target plus adjacent targets.

Area of Effect

All legal targets within an affected group.

Bounce

Initial target selected by player; subsequent hits distributed according to ability rules.

Lane

Affects Front and/or Back units sharing a lane.

Row

Affects an entire Front or Back Row.

Random

Engine selects legal targets.

Self

Source only.

Ally

One or more friendly targets.

Global

All combatants meeting defined criteria.

18

Target Selection UX

Selecting an action enters a targeting state.

Valid targets become visually obvious.

Invalid targets dim.

Hovering a target can expose:

  • Name
  • Position
  • HP
  • Toughness
  • Weaknesses
  • Relevant conditions
  • Expected targeting pattern
  • Break potential
  • Important ability warnings

Example:

Murder Drone — FRL

HP: ██████░░

Toughness: ◇◇

Weakness: ⚡ ❄️

BREAK POSSIBLE

The engine does not require the player to understand why an illegal target is illegal before making the action.

It communicates legal choices directly.

19

Action Wheel

The primary combat control is a contextual radial / partial-radial action menu.

The initial wheel expands approximately from 12 o'clock through 5–6 o'clock, leaving the hero art unobscured.

Potential top-level actions:

  • Attack
  • Skill
  • Ultimate
  • Item
  • Defend
  • Special
  • Character-specific action

Selecting a category expands its available actions.

Example:

ATTACK

→ Basic Attack

→ Skill

→ Alternate Attack

→ Character-specific option

The wheel is not identical for every character.

A summoner may expose Command.

A stance fighter may expose Stance.

A hacker may expose Hack.

A character with deployables may expose Deploy.

The action wheel belongs to the character, not merely to the rules engine.

Weakness and Toughness

Enemies may possess:

  • HP
  • Toughness
  • One or more elemental Weaknesses

Attacking an enemy using an appropriate Element reduces Toughness.

Foundry handles the calculation.

Players see the Toughness bar change immediately.

Example:

◇◇◇◇ → ◇◇

When Toughness reaches zero:

WEAKNESS BREAK

The engine resolves the appropriate Break effect.

21

Break Effects

Break may cause:

  • Bonus damage
  • Timeline delay
  • Element-specific status
  • Vulnerability
  • Ability interruption
  • Boss mechanic disruption
  • Exposure of hidden targets
  • Phase interaction

Elements should differ meaningfully in how Break behaves.

Element should therefore be more than damage color.

24

Trigger Stack / Reaction Resolver

Multiple abilities may react to the same event.

The engine therefore requires a deterministic reaction system.

Example:

Enemy attacks Ally A.

  1. Damage is declared.
  2. Defender reaction becomes available.
  3. Preservation ally may intercept.
  4. Shield response resolves.
  5. Damage resolves.
  6. HP-loss Talents detect the result.
  7. Healing reaction may fire.
  8. Resource gains resolve.
  9. Follow-up eligibility is checked.

The player should never see an incomprehensible technical stack.

They see only the decisions relevant to them.

25

Follow-Up Attacks

Follow-up attacks are first-class actions.

They may trigger when:

  • An ally attacks
  • An ally is damaged
  • An enemy is Broken
  • A marked enemy acts
  • A shield is attacked
  • Healing occurs
  • A summon acts
  • A condition is applied
  • Another specific trigger occurs

Follow-ups may be:

  • Automatic
  • Optional
  • Limited per round / cycle
  • Resource-gated

The system tracks limits and eligibility.

26

Reactions and Interrupts

Reaction prompts appear contextually.

Example:

ALLY TARGETED

INTERVENE?

[Shield] [Counter] [Ignore]

The prompt:

  • Identifies the event
  • Shows the affected character
  • Shows available responses
  • Enforces timing
  • Resolves the selected effect
  • Disappears when no longer legal

Players should not need to shout over one another asking whether they can react.

27

Summons / Companions / Reflections

Summoned entities are first-class combat participants.

They may have:

  • Their own artwork
  • HP or Life
  • Actions
  • Timeline position
  • Duration
  • Resources
  • Status effects
  • Triggered abilities
  • Relationship to summoner statistics

The engine must support summons that:

  • Act independently
  • Act immediately
  • Share statistics with owner
  • Use owner's statistics
  • Have unique statistics
  • Consume their own Life
  • Disappear after a timer
  • Transform
  • Detonate
  • Trigger events when entering/leaving play

30

Advancement / Skill Tree

Advancement should be visible.

The sheet includes an interactive progression spine / tree.

Possible categories:

  • Basic
  • Skill
  • Ultimate
  • Talent
  • Technique
  • Signature mechanic
  • Major Traits
  • Minor Traits
  • Attribute improvements

Nodes visibly change when purchased.

Whenever possible, advancement should create new behavior, not merely increase percentages.

Bad advancement:

Damage +3%.

Better advancement:

The first time you Break an enemy each encounter, immediately Advance 15 on the timeline.

Progression should make the character more interesting, not just numerically larger.

31

Character Creation

The game is built around original player characters.

Canon characters may exist in the world, but PCs are not expected to play them.

Character creation must eventually support:

  1. Concept
  2. Origin / Homeworld
  3. Way
  4. Element
  5. Attributes
  6. Skills
  7. Background Traits
  8. Combat kit
  9. Signature mechanic
  10. Starting advancement
  11. Equipment
  12. Bonds / connections
  13. Portrait and visual identity

The difficult design problem is allowing bespoke-feeling characters without requiring players to program their own Foundry actors.

This will require a structured Hero Builder.

Way is not a rigid class.

It may influence:

  • Available Talents
  • Advancement options
  • Signature-engine families
  • Ultimate styles
  • Combat role
  • Equipment interaction

Two characters of the same Way should not be assumed to have the same rotation.

34

Hero Builder

Because bespoke characters are a central appeal, the system requires a player-facing kit construction system.

The builder should provide controlled components for:

  • Basic Attack
  • Skill
  • Ultimate
  • Talent
  • Technique
  • Personal resource
  • Trigger
  • Targeting pattern
  • Costs
  • Effects
  • Advancement branches

Way and Element constrain available combinations sufficiently to protect balance and identity.

The player creates a distinctive hero without writing formulas or code.

An advanced GM mode may permit unrestricted custom powers.

Combat Transition

When hostilities begin:

  1. Exploration UI recedes.
  2. Combatants are identified.
  3. Battle Stage loads.
  4. Enemy formation initializes.
  5. Character art enters.
  6. Timeline appears.
  7. Skill Point pool appears.
  8. Weakness/Toughness data activates.
  9. Opening Techniques resolve.
  10. Combat begins.

When combat ends:

  1. Rewards resolve.
  2. Temporary combat states clean up.
  3. Summons dismiss as appropriate.
  4. Exploration state returns.
  5. Persistent consequences remain.

Development Methodology

Every major system is developed through explicit phases.

Full Plan First

Before a large feature begins:

  • Define desired player experience
  • Define data required
  • Define affected systems
  • Break implementation into phases
  • Identify likely integration risks
  • Establish test cases

One Phase at a Time

Do not build the battle renderer, timeline, Break engine, reaction engine, and character resources simultaneously.

Each feature earns its way into the system.

Layered Testing

Layer 1 — Feature Test

Does the thing itself work?

Layer 2 — Integration Test

Does it still work when interacting with every neighboring system?

Layer 3 — End-to-End Test

Can the entire intended sequence execute correctly?

Layer 4 — Live Play

Does any of this actually feel good with four human beings trying to break it?

A feature is not finished because it works in an isolated developer test.

Success Criteria

The project succeeds if a session can produce this experience:

The players begin aboard their ship.

They arrive at a new world.

They wander into town.

They spend half an hour arguing with a shopkeeper.

One character gets into trouble at the fountain.

Another discovers an actual lead.

They investigate.

The GM never needs a tactical grid.

The party enters an abandoned facility.

Something attacks.

The screen changes.

Their heroes step into the foreground.

Enemies settle into Front and Back positions.

The timeline lights.

The action wheel opens.

A player spends the party's last Skill Point.

Another realizes they now have to Basic Attack to restore one.

An enemy prepares a devastating move.

The party desperately Breaks it before its timeline marker arrives.

Someone's Ultimate becomes ready.

They interrupt.

A summon enters the fight.

A reaction triggers.

The boss changes phase.

The players win because of decisions they made, not because the computer played for them.

The battle UI fades.

They return to town.

The character who started the fountain fight is still wet.

The party spends the remaining hour roleplaying.

That is the target experience.

The Fundamental Rule

Every major feature should be judged against one question:

Does this make the people at the table feel more like they are living an adventure together?

If automation removes bookkeeping, keep it.

If presentation communicates what is happening, keep it.

If a mechanic creates a meaningful decision, keep it.

If a rule exists only because another RPG traditionally has that rule, question it.

If a videogame mechanic becomes tedious when transferred to tabletop, redesign it.

If something is visually impressive but slows the game without adding meaning, remove it.

If Foundry can make an impossible paper mechanic effortless and understandable, exploit that advantage mercilessly.

Project Statement

This project exists to build a type of tabletop RPG that rarely gets attempted:

a campaign-scale roleplaying game whose tabletop rules, user interface, combat presentation, automation, character design, and GM tools are conceived as one system rather than separate products.

The goal is not to make Foundry imitate a videogame.

The goal is to ask what a tabletop RPG can become when it knows from its first line of design that a computer is sitting beneath the table.

Players still talk.

Players still improvise.

Players still make characters.

Players still surprise the GM.

Players still decide what matters.

Players still roll dice.

The machine simply handles the parts that were never fun to do by hand.

The world belongs to the table.

The rules support it.

Foundry makes it move.
