The Sandpoint Frontier

Small town · big wilderness · trouble close to home

Sandpoint is not a great city. It is a small frontier settlement surrounded by farms, rough country, wooded hills, and more wilderness than anyone in town can comfortably control.

The trouble began with livestock disappearing from the outlying farms. Then barns and storehouses were raided. Then children began to vanish.

Everyone blames goblins. They have been seen in the wooded hills beyond the hinterlands, but nobody knows how many there are, how organized they have become, or where their chief keeps his lair. What everyone agrees on is simpler: the raids are getting bolder.

What Your Character Knows

Goblins are hitting the farms around Sandpoint. At least one child may still be alive somewhere in their territory. Goblin country begins roughly half a day's walk from town. A larger settlement with a proper armoury is about two days away—too far to solve every equipment problem at a moment's notice.

The First Job

The adventure starts with a simple offer

Mayor Kendra Devron has little money, fewer soldiers, and no one else volunteering to crawl into goblin holes.

She offers your company 100 gold pieces to clear a tunnel complex in the hills half a day's walk from Sandpoint and bring home anyone still alive inside.

She has a crude map showing the hillside and the tunnel mouth. She does not know how many goblins are there or how deep the tunnels run.

Go in. Clear them out. Search for captives. Come home.

That is all your characters need to know when play begins.

Sandpoint

Your home base between expeditions

Sandpoint is poor enough that treasure matters and small enough that people remember what you do. If you save a family, people know. If you hire someone from the tavern and come home without them, people know that too.

Town Hall

Mayor Devron's office and a map increasingly marked with raids, missing livestock, and trouble in the hinterlands.

Market

Sell gems, curiosities, salvage, and whatever strange things you drag back. Weapons and armour are harder to replace.

Smith

Competent, slow, and only one forge. Broken gear can be repaired. New gear takes time.

Shrine

Ordinary religious services, offerings, and memorials for people who do not come back from the hills.

Tavern

Porters, torchbearers, hunters, men-at-arms, and would-be adventurers sometimes looking for paid work.

Hinterlands

The farms that feed Sandpoint—and the first places to hear screaming when something comes out of the woods.

What Kind of Game Is This?

Old-school danger without a predetermined story

This is a low-level Dragonslayer adventure. You begin at 1st level. The wilderness is dangerous, dungeon time matters, light matters, and combat can become lethal very quickly.

That does not mean every problem is a fight. Enemies use morale. Creatures may flee or surrender. Retreat is a real option. Good plans, caution, negotiation, ambushes, traps, hirelings, and ugly improvisation can matter as much as a high attack roll.

  • No class is mandatory. Thieves, clerics, clever readers, and sturdy fighters all have obvious uses, but the adventure does not require a particular party composition.
  • Your decisions carry forward. People you save stay saved. Enemies you let escape may remember. Sandpoint reacts to what happens.
  • Failure changes the situation. Losing a fight, retreating, or missing an objective does not automatically end the campaign.
  • Death is possible. This is dangerous 1st-level adventuring. Make a character you want to keep, but do not assume the world is obliged to keep them alive.
  • Practical play is rewarded. Ask questions. Look at the room. Use the terrain. Bring light. Keep track of time. Decide when something is not worth touching.

Rules Worth Knowing

The campaign rulings that will affect your decisions most often

  1. Initiative is rolled every round. Side order can swing back and forth quickly. Do not assume today's turn order survives into the next round.
  2. Declare spells before initiative. A caster states the spell and target before anyone knows which side acts first.
  3. Zero hit points is not automatically death. At exactly 0 hp you are unconscious for 1d6 rounds, then return at 1 hp. Below −10 is death. Dying characters save versus death; a natural 20 is a miracle and returns the character to 1 hp.
  4. Enemies have morale. Half a group going down, a leader falling, or something terrifying happening can make enemies run. Not everything fights to the death.
  5. Binding wounds works; sleeping does not heal you. Ten minutes after a fight restores 1d3 hp. A night's sleep restores time and spells, but no hp by itself.
  6. Time creates danger. Long searches, stubborn locks, resting, forcing doors, and lingering in hostile places can trigger encounter checks.
  7. Natural 1s create openings. A character may drop or compromise a weapon; bows can lose a string. When a monster rolls a natural 1 in melee, its target gets an immediate free attack.
  8. Advantage and disadvantage are ±4. Smaller situational benefits can be worth +1 or +2 when the circumstances clearly support them.
  9. Dangerous ideas are allowed to work. A wild stunt may cost position, hp, an ability check, or expose you to a save—but if you accept the risk and succeed, expect a real effect.

Before You Leave Town

A useful five-minute party conversation

  • Who is carrying the light?
  • Who has food?
  • Who has a backup weapon?
  • Who can carry treasure or a wounded companion?
  • Are you hiring a porter or torchbearer?
  • Are you hiring someone who can actually fight?
  • Who usually takes point underground?
  • Who usually speaks for the group in town?
  • What would make your character retreat?
  • What would make your character keep going?

One Last Thing

You do not need to solve the campaign before you begin it. You need a 1st-level adventurer, a reason to take dangerous work, and enough sense to understand that a dark hole in the hills can contain things nobody in Sandpoint knows about.
