TALLOW — Albert’s sight & light tab

TALLOW

Sight and light for a dungeon crawl — built, live-tested, and in play.

Creatures are hidden. The map never is. Every wall, label and inked line stays fully legible at all times; the only thing that goes away is what is alive on it.

The purpose

A dungeon crawl is a game about not knowing what is around the corner. Foundry can give you that, but it charges for it in the one currency a hand-drawn map cannot spare: it hides the map to hide the monster. Half the table is then looking at a black wedge, which tells them nothing, and the art nobody can see may as well not have been drawn.

TALLOW takes the tension without the blindfold. A player watching a goblin appear as it steps into their torchlight is being told something true. A player staring at a black wedge is only being told that the software is working.

The theory

Almost every VTT vision system asks “what is visible from here?” That is a region query. Its answer is a shape — and a shape has to be painted, and what gets painted is what covers your map. The blackout is not a design choice anybody made; it falls out of the question being asked.

TALLOW asks a different question: “can A see B?” That is a boolean. It is asked once per creature per client, and its answer needs nothing painted at all. Everything else in the system is a consequence of that one substitution.

Because the answer is per-pair and per-client, it can be honest about disagreement: the same goblin is visible on the dwarf's screen and absent on the human's, at the same instant, with no second scene and nothing synchronised between them.

It also runs backwards. If a torch is a thing that adds a yes, then fog and magical darkness are things that add a no — the same point, reach and colour, dragged on the same way, drawn with the same wall-hugging bubble. Nothing about the engine had to change to take sight away, which is the clearest sign the question was the right one.

The scene ships with Foundry's own vision off. That is not tidiness — with vision off, core computes no sight at all, so TALLOW is not layered on top of Foundry's system. It replaces it.

How it works

  1. The rule is a pure function, tested in isolation, that takes one observer and one target and returns yes or no. Nothing about Foundry appears in it.
  2. The client asks it for every creature, roughly ten times a second, and hands the answer to Foundry as a visibility override. A GM is exempt and sees everything.
  3. Light is public, sense is private. Standing in any pool makes you visible to everyone with a clear line, at any distance. Darkvision shows a creature only to the one whose eyes they are.
  4. Walls are consulted once, by the same polygon sweep, for the light test and the sight test alike. A pillar in open floor hides you — no rooms, no zones, no authoring.
  5. The bubbles explain it. A rippling amber edge is firelight, a slate one is a sense. They are clamped to the real sight polygon, so a pillar takes a visible bite out of them: the shadow behind it is simply outside the shape.

The soft edge is deliberate. A hard circle reads as a rule being enforced; a moving one reads as perception. Where a wall cuts the bubble the edge goes still — because how far you can see is a soft thing, and a wall is not.

Built

  • The rule & the engine — per-client concealment, walls, range, light, force-reveal, and a GM “see as” that looks through one character's eyes.
  • Darkvision by race and by creature, off in the light, tunable per race without editing content files.
  • Every burning thing — carried torch, lantern and candle; standing campfires, braziers and sconces dragged onto the map; an enemy's own light.
  • The bubbles — one geometry, one clock, two colours, clamped to the walls.
  • Chat concealment — an unseen attacker's card arrives nameless, with the dice and the speaker stripped.
  • This panel — who holds what, what burns where, every distance in one place, and the scene's own look.
  • Ignite / Douse on the item itself, so a player strikes their own light without asking the GM.
  • Fog and magical darkness — the reverse of a torch. Fog cuts you to one square in every direction and hides whoever stands in it; darkness is absolute, and neither a torch nor darkvision reaches into it. Both are VOLUMES: they flood outward a square at a time and roll around corners instead of casting a shadow of themselves, and a torch stops exactly where they are drawn to begin.
  • Burn-down — a light that gutters out on its own, and fuel spent by the hour rather than by the click. The clock is decorative until this lands.
  • Spell light — Light, Continual Light and Darkness are listed below and read-only, because no spell casts light yet.
The book decides. Torch 30 ft, lantern 30 ft on a flask of oil every two hours, candle 5 ft — and darkvision that works “in total darkness,” which is why firelight switches it off. Where the rulebook is silent the invention is marked as ours, on the page and in the code: creatures have no printed sight range anywhere, so the 60 ft default below is a number this system had to make up.

Who is carrying what, what stands burning on the map, what is blinding people, and how far any of it reaches. A lit character is visible to everything with a clear line to them — that is the trade, and the amber rows are the ones paying it.

A dashed field is still the book's own number; a solid one is a house rule you have set, and the border changes the moment you set it — so a rule you have bent can never hide. Changing a race writes to the race item, the copy on every character, and the compendium: all three, or the change would not show. Changing a carried light shortens every one already burning. The creature default is always solid, because the rulebook gives creatures no sight range anywhere and 60 ft is a number this system had to invent. Spells are read-only until one of them actually casts light.
